The Price of Belonging

September 10, 2026 · 19 min read

#notes#morocco#belonging#community#boundaries#family#identity#culture#personal

A child could belong to an entire neighborhood.

The adults did not need to be your parents to feed you, correct you, ask where you were going, or send you home when the hour became late. At a wedding, nobody stood outside the work. Chairs appeared. Food moved through many hands. Music started somewhere and the whole place answered. A family event became a street, then a neighborhood, then a crowd in which the line between guest and relative was no longer useful.

Care did not wait for an invitation.

Neither did authority.

The same neighbor who could treat you like his own child could also believe this gave him the right to judge the adult you became. The same community that would carry your family through grief could turn one private decision into a public case. People who had watched you grow did not always experience your life as something belonging entirely to you. They had contributed to it. They remembered you before you remembered yourself. Some of them believed this made them shareholders.

Nobody says this when you are young.

The tribe never sends you a bill for belonging.

It collects through the parts of yourself you are expected not to become.

The safety net and the cage were built from the same material#

When I use the word tribe, I do not mean primitive, irrational, or frozen in the past. I do not mean only a literal tribe, or only Amazigh communities, or some clean category that contains Morocco and excludes the rest of the world.

I mean a way of organizing life in which the person is understood through the group before he is understood alone. Family, neighborhood, origin, reputation, and inherited obligation form the structure around the individual. Your choices do not end at your skin because their consequences are believed to travel through everyone whose name is attached to yours.

That structure can keep a person alive.

When institutions are distant, expensive, or untrustworthy, the tribe becomes the institution. It is childcare, insurance, transportation, employment, conflict resolution, food in a difficult month, and people filling a room when death enters the family. It remembers who your grandparents were. It preserves the language, songs, recipes, stories, and forms of care that official systems often consider too ordinary to record.

The communal world of the Souss helped keep Amazigh life present even when schools and institutions gave its language less authority. The wedding carried what the textbook did not. The neighborhood transmitted what the archive failed to keep. A culture survives because people repeat it together.

I do not want to describe that world only as a cage. That would be dishonest and ungrateful. Some of the strongest parts of me were built inside its care.

But a safety net and a cage can be woven from the same material.

The closeness that makes it difficult for you to fall can also make it difficult for you to move. The people who will never leave you alone in a crisis may also refuse to leave you alone with a decision. The community that knows when you need help also knows when you have departed from the expected shape.

Belonging protects you from disappearance.

It can also protect the group from your difference.

In one world, I was a person. In the other, I was a relationship#

In America, I encountered a different story about the self.

The person came first. What do you want to study? Where do you want to live? What do you believe? The language assumed that a life could be separated into individual choices, and that the person making them had the highest authority over their meaning.

I know reality does not always honor that promise. Money can decide before the individual does. Employers, immigration systems, race, class, illness, and loneliness can narrow a life while the culture continues calling it free. American individualism sometimes abandons people and renames the abandonment independence.

Still, the assumption changed something for me.

A decision could be mine without first becoming an argument about everybody connected to me. A preference did not always require a genealogy. A boundary could be heard as information about the person drawing it rather than an insult to the person standing outside it.

Where I came from, I was rarely only an individual.

I was a son, a grandson, a relative, a neighbor, a person from a particular place. These were not descriptions added after the person. They were the person. To behave was to behave as somebody’s son. To succeed was to raise the family. To fail was to become evidence attached to more names than my own.

This gives achievement warmth. A success does not remain lonely. Everyone who helped raise you can feel some part of it returning to them.

It also gives deviation an audience.

A choice can embarrass people who did not make it. A mistake can become a stain on somebody who was not in the room. A life designed around your own convictions can be treated as theft from the collective story.

In one world, I was asked to become myself.

In the other, I was asked to remain recognizable.

Neither request is simple. But only one of them can interpret transformation as betrayal.

“No” begins an investigation#

People like to say that no is a complete sentence.

That sentence was not written inside a tribe.

Inside a tribe, no is the beginning of the discussion. Why not? Who told you to say that? Is somebody turning you against us? Do you think you are better than everyone? What will people say? After everything we have done for you, how can you refuse this one thing?

The boundary does not remain between two people.

One person tells another. An aunt calls a parent. A parent calls a sibling. A private limit enters the family network and returns carrying the weight of a collective verdict. By the time it reaches you again, you are no longer deciding whether to accept one request. You are deciding whether to disturb the peace of an entire system.

This is why boundaries can look easy from the outside.

Say no. Stop answering. Leave. Protect your peace.

The advice imagines relationships as separate rooms with separate doors. Close one and the others remain unchanged. But inside a tightly connected community, the rooms share walls. Closing one door can darken the whole house. Distance from one person may become an accusation against your parents. Refusing one obligation may change how relatives treat your siblings. A private act of self-protection can produce public consequences for people you still love.

The difficulty is not that the person drawing the boundary does not understand the word no.

The difficulty is that he understands its price.

Gratitude can become a claim of ownership#

The tribe’s strongest argument is usually true.

You did not raise yourself.

Other people fed you, watched you, corrected you, carried you, opened doors, absorbed your mistakes, and made sacrifices you did not understand when you received them. The independent adult is standing on years of dependence. Any account of the self that deletes those years is fiction.

I believe in gratitude for this reason. I owe more than I can name to people whose care arrived before I had the language to recognize it.

But gratitude becomes dangerous when it is converted into ownership.

We raised you, therefore we choose your future.

We protected you, therefore you cannot create distance from us.

We sacrificed for you, therefore your life must repay the sacrifice in the form we prefer.

The original care may have been real. That does not make every later demand legitimate. A gift does not become less generous because the receiver grows beyond the giver’s imagination. Parenting, family, and community cannot be loans whose hidden interest is control over an adult life.

This is one reason the argument is so painful. Refusing control can feel like denying the care that came before it. The person setting the boundary is forced to defend two truths at once:

What you gave me mattered.

What you gave me does not make me yours.

Many relationships cannot hold both sentences. They demand that gratitude take the shape of obedience, then describe every other shape as ingratitude.

The result is a debt that can never be paid. Every payment proves that another payment can be requested. Every sacrifice becomes evidence that you are still a good member of the group. The day you stop, the entire history is reopened as if none of the earlier payments counted.

That is not gratitude.

It is a mortgage placed on a person.

Shame is how the tribe enters the room before everyone else#

The tribe does not need to be physically present to enforce itself.

It teaches you to carry the audience inside your head.

What will people say?

The question sounds like concern about reputation, but it does something larger. It places an invisible crowd inside a private decision. Before you ask whether the choice is right, kind, honest, or necessary, you imagine how it will look after traveling through people who do not know its reasons.

The imagined audience is powerful because it cannot be answered. People have no single face, no exact objection, and no point at which they become satisfied. You cannot explain yourself to them. You can only anticipate them.

So you begin editing before anybody speaks.

You reduce the ambition to avoid appearing arrogant. You stay in the role to avoid appearing selfish. You tolerate the intrusion to avoid appearing cold. You hide the relationship, the failure, the doubt, the success, or the part of yourself whose existence would create too many conversations.

Eventually, surveillance no longer requires a watcher.

The person watches himself.

This is the cheapest form of social control. Nobody has to stop you because the possible judgment has already entered the planning stage. You choose from the set of lives least likely to require an explanation, then experience the choice as your own.

Shame can protect a community from cruelty. The knowledge that our actions affect other people is part of becoming human. A culture with no social shame does not produce freedom; it produces people who treat appetite as innocence.

But shame becomes destructive when it protects appearances instead of people.

If a family would rather preserve the image of harmony than confront the person causing harm, shame is guarding the wrong thing. If a community fears the public knowledge of an injury more than the injury itself, reputation has replaced morality. If the question is always how the truth will look and never what the truth is, the audience has become more real than the human being standing in front of it.

Every tribe has an approved version of you#

The approved version is usually not miserable.

He can be educated, successful, generous, even unusual. The tribe may celebrate him as long as his difference increases the group’s status without weakening its authority. His success must return as proof of the family, the neighborhood, or the culture. He may travel, but he must remain available. He may become independent, but not in a way that removes anybody’s access. He may change, but he should continue performing the older version whenever he returns.

This is how a community can be proud of your achievement and threatened by the person the achievement required.

They like the title and resent the hours it takes to earn it. They celebrate the result and punish the concentration. They tell the story of how far you traveled and become uncomfortable when the distance changes what you can tolerate.

In Support Has a Timestamp, I wrote about people who create obstacles during the uncertain years and arrive with congratulations after the outcome becomes visible. Tribal belonging adds another layer to that contradiction. The person who made the journey harder may still feel entitled to the ending because the relationship existed before the work.

Your achievement becomes communal property.

Your boundary remains a private offense.

The tribe accepts the version of change it can display. The quieter changes are harder: the ability to say no, the refusal to confuse age with correctness, the decision not to make yourself available for every crisis, the realization that love does not require permanent exposure to harm.

Those changes cannot be placed in a photograph. They alter the distribution of power.

That is why they are often called disrespect.

“You changed” is both an accusation and a fact#

Of course I changed.

Life should leave evidence.

I crossed languages, institutions, fields, countries, and rooms that required me to rebuild parts of my understanding. I encountered people whose lives were organized around different assumptions. I learned that some rules I had treated as laws were local customs, some duties were fears inherited without inspection, and some impossibilities were only things nobody near me had attempted.

Returning unchanged would be stranger than changing.

But when somebody says you changed, they are rarely making a neutral observation. They mean the old methods no longer produce the old response. The guilt does not work at the same speed. The interruption is no longer granted automatic priority. The role remains available, but the actor has begun reading the script.

Change reveals agreements that were invisible while you obeyed them.

You discover that the peace depended on your silence only after you speak. You discover that the closeness depended on unlimited access only after you close a door. You discover that the love contained a role only after you grow beyond it.

The accusation still hurts because part of me wants to remain recognizable to the people who knew me first. There is a specific grief in becoming difficult for your own origin to understand. Strangers can misunderstand the adult and lose nothing. The people from home remember the child. Their confusion can make growth feel like a form of disappearance.

So I have sometimes made myself smaller during the return.

I have translated the person I became into the version least likely to disturb the room. I have confused making others comfortable with remaining authentic. I have treated their recognition as proof that I had not traveled too far.

But a home that recognizes only the person who left is asking the person who returned to vanish.

America did not solve belonging#

Individualism can make boundaries easier because it weakens the collective claim.

It can also make the boundary unnecessary by removing the relationship entirely.

In America, privacy is often respected because nobody expects to know. People can live beside one another for years without acquiring the authority, or the responsibility, that a Moroccan neighbor may assume in a week. Freedom expands. So does solitude.

There is relief in not being watched.

There is also a day when nobody notices you need food.

The individual can reinvent himself, but he may have to finance, explain, and survive the reinvention alone. The culture protects his right to choose while offering no guarantee that anybody will catch him if the choice fails. The tribe can ask for too much because it gives so much. Individualism can ask for nothing because it promises nothing.

I do not want to trade one simplification for another. Morocco is not one family, and America is not one personality. There are lonely Moroccan homes and controlling American ones. There are families in Morocco that make room for difference, and communities in America that understand belonging as a serious obligation. Geography changes the dominant language of the relationship; it does not determine every relationship inside it.

Still, the contrast helped me see what each world had made invisible.

The tribe taught me that no person is self-made.

Individualism taught me that no contribution makes a person collectively owned.

I need both lessons.

A boundary inside a tribe is an act of translation#

The first boundaries I understood were built in the language of separation.

My time. My decision. My privacy. My life.

Each sentence was true. Each one could sound like a declaration of war inside a world organized around ours.

I had to learn that drawing a boundary inside a tribe requires more than importing the vocabulary of individualism. It requires translating self-respect into a language that does not deny relationship.

I am not refusing because you mean nothing to me.

I am refusing because remaining close in this form is destroying the part of me capable of loving you without resentment.

I am not keeping this private because I am ashamed of you.

I am keeping it private because intimacy stops being intimacy when it is distributed without consent.

I am not choosing my work because I believe I am better than the family.

I am choosing it because a life cannot serve anybody well if it is built entirely from abandoned convictions.

Translation does not guarantee acceptance. Sometimes the group understands the boundary perfectly and rejects it because understanding was never the problem. The problem was losing access.

Then the boundary has to survive without agreement.

That may mean sharing less, leaving earlier, refusing to relitigate the decision, or accepting that somebody will tell a story in which your self-preservation is cruelty. It may mean carrying the discomfort of being misunderstood instead of returning to the behavior that made you understandable.

Inside a tribe, a boundary is never drawn around only two people. Everybody believes they are standing on the line.

The line still has to exist.

Belonging without ownership#

I do not want a life in which nobody can ask anything of me.

That is not freedom. It is disconnection with good branding.

I want obligations. I want to be the person who arrives when somebody I love is in trouble. I want the child in the neighborhood to experience adults beyond his parents as sources of safety. I want weddings that require many hands, grief that does not leave one family alone in a room, food that moves toward whoever needs it, and languages kept alive because people still use them to care for one another.

I want the tribe’s memory.

I do not want its ownership.

The distinction is consent. An obligation can be real without being unlimited. Care can create gratitude without purchasing control. A community can advise, warn, disagree, and remain present without treating disagreement as exile. A person can carry his family forward without being forced to carry every fear the family inherited.

This requires the tribe to survive a member it cannot completely recognize.

It requires parents to accept that raising a child successfully may produce an adult whose decisions they would never make. It requires the community to see a boundary not only as something being taken away, but as an attempt to preserve a relationship at a distance where it can remain honest.

It also requires the individual to resist the flattering lie of total self-creation. Setting a boundary does not erase what was given. Becoming different does not make the people who stayed behind smaller. Freedom should not require contempt for the world that formed you.

The goal is not to escape every claim.

It is to make the claims speak their names.

Is this care, or control?

Is this obligation chosen through love, or obedience extracted through shame?

Am I protecting a person, or protecting the community from having to explain that person?

Does belonging mean that I will not abandon you, or that you are not allowed to change beyond me?

The tribe becomes safer when it can answer those questions without treating the questions themselves as betrayal.

The price should not be the self#

Belonging will always cost something.

It costs time, compromise, patience, responsibility, and the inconvenience of making room for lives that do not move according to your schedule. Any community that costs nothing is probably an audience, not a community.

I am willing to pay that price.

I am not willing to pay with the authorship of my life.

The people around me can be part of my decisions without becoming their owners. They can remind me where I came from without deciding how far I am allowed to go. I can honor the people who raised me without living inside every expectation they have of me.

For years, I thought the choice was between belonging and becoming myself.

Choose belonging, and remain loved in a shape that no longer fits.

Choose yourself, and become the person who abandoned his people.

I no longer accept the choice.

If belonging requires my disappearance, then the tribe is not keeping me. It is keeping a memory of me and asking the living person to maintain it.

Real belonging has to recognize return, not only sameness. It has to make room for the person transformed by distance, education, failure, work, other cultures, and time. It has to trust that difference is not always rejection, and that a boundary can be an attempt to stay without surrendering.

The world that raised me gave me more than I can repay.

It gave me language, rhythm, memory, food, people who could enter the house without becoming guests, and the knowledge that a human being should not face every difficulty alone.

I want to keep all of it.

I only refuse the hidden condition that says keeping it requires me to remain the person everybody else remembers.

The price of belonging should be what we willingly carry for one another.

It should never be the right to become ourselves.
