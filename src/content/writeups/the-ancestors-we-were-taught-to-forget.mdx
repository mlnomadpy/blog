A conqueror has not finished when the last defender falls. The land can still
remember. The mountains keep the old names. The mothers keep the old words. The
food keeps the seasons, the jewelry keeps the symbols, the weddings keep the
rhythms, and the children keep asking who built the graves on the hill. A people
can lose a war and remain dangerously present inside everything that comes
after it.

So the final battle is fought later, in the memory of the grandchildren.

Teach them that their history began when you arrived. Give their ancestors
smaller names. Call resistance savagery and conquest civilization. Take the
woman who led armies against you and preserve her only through a title that
makes her sound like a sorceress. Make the native language feel provincial in
the mouth and the imported language feel educated. Let a few generations pass,
and the descendants will do the rest themselves. They will correct their own
mothers. They will hide the names of their villages. They will search for an
ancestor from somewhere far away because the stranger's blood has been made to
feel cleaner than their own.

Then the conquest is complete. The conquered person will stand on the grave of
his ancestor and swear he comes from somewhere else.

## The winner does not write history alone. He edits the loser out

People say history is written by the winners as if it means the winner gets the
better paragraph. It is worse than that. The winner decides what counts as an
archive. His letters become documents; your songs become folklore. His general
has a strategy; your fighter has an impulse. His violence has dates, motives,
and footnotes; yours becomes a trait of character. He gets to be complicated.
You become the obstacle he overcame on the way to the next chapter.

And every winner needs a past that makes his victory look deserved. Power does
not like to remember itself arriving. It prefers to discover that it was always
there, that the land had been waiting for it, that what came before was only a
confused preface. So it builds a narrative in which conquest was rescue,
replacement was progress, and the people already under its feet were either not
really there or not really a people.

That is the whitewashing of history in its literal sense: not merely telling a
lie, but applying the lie in coats until no earlier color can come through.
First the names disappear from the books. Then the language disappears from the
school. Then the child disappears from his own lineage. By the end, nobody has
to burn the archive. The archive is alive, walking around, embarrassed by
itself.

Morocco knows this process well because Morocco has known many arrivals. The
coast and mountains have seen empires and armies come under different flags:
Roman, Byzantine, Arab, French, Spanish. They did not all rule in the same way,
for the same length, or leave the same wound, and history becomes useless if we
flatten every encounter into one event. But power learned the same lesson each
time. The cheapest way to own a land for longer than your soldiers can remain
is to teach its people to narrate themselves through you.

No story shows the machinery more clearly than the woman we remember as Dihya.

## The woman left behind as a name given by her enemy

Near the end of the seventh century, an Amazigh leader in the Aurès resisted
the armies moving west across North Africa. Later Arabic chronicles remembered
her as *al-Kahina*: the diviner, the seer, the woman with forbidden knowledge.
Today many call her Dihya. She appears as queen, military leader, mother,
prophetess, tyrant, heroine, Jew, Christian, or pagan, depending on who is
telling the story and what that storyteller needs from her.

Even the basic facts reach us through fog. The surviving written accounts were
set down after her life, largely inside the tradition of the side that defeated
her. [Modern historians still argue](https://doi.org/10.1080/2222582X.2013.830411)
over her alliances, her faith, the tribes she led, and how much of the famous
story was built by later memory. A careful history has to admit that uncertainty.
But uncertainty is not emptiness. Beneath the legends, scholars still find a
North African woman who held political and military power and led resistance to
the Umayyad advance before she was defeated around the turn of the eighth
century.

Think about what it means that we meet her this way. A woman resists conquest,
and history hands her back to us wearing the conqueror's vocabulary. We know the
epithet more easily than we know her own name. We inherit pages about what her
enemies thought she was, while almost nothing survives of what she called
herself, what language she used when she gave an order, what her people believed
they were defending, or what they said when they buried their dead.

This is how a winner creates a narrative that serves his image. He does not have
to erase the defeated leader completely. Sometimes keeping her is more useful.
He keeps her as proof that the land was wild, superstitious, divided, waiting
for history to begin. Her resistance becomes the darkness that makes his arrival
look like light.

But Dihya survived the frame. That is the part I love. The archive tried to keep
the name as a warning and people turned it into an inheritance. The figure that
was supposed to mark the end of Amazigh resistance became evidence that there
had been something organized, rooted, and powerful enough to resist. The story
could be bent, but it could not be made to disappear.

And that leaves a harder question for Morocco now. If Dihya could survive more
than thirteen centuries in the memory, why do so many of her possible
descendants find it difficult to say the word Amazigh about themselves?

## Dihya was not standing alone

The danger of recovering one great figure is that the exception can be used to
hide the rule. Give the forgotten people one queen, turn her into a statue, and
the rest of the history can remain empty. Dihya becomes a miraculous interruption
in a land otherwise waiting to be acted upon. We remember the warrior and still
forget the people capable of producing her.

But she was not standing alone, and she did not come from nowhere.

Centuries before her, North Africa had already produced kingdoms that negotiated,
fought, traded, and betrayed with the powers around the Mediterranean. Massinissa
built Numidia into a major kingdom in the second century before our era. He was
not a simple hero in a clean national story; he allied with Rome against Carthage,
and power was complicated then for the same reason it is complicated now. But he
matters because he destroys the picture of ancient North Africans as scattered
tribes with no political imagination until somebody civilized them. There was a
state here. There was diplomacy here. There were ambitions, institutions, armies,
and people making choices inside a world larger than the village.

Then came Jugurtha, Massinissa's grandson, whose war against Rome lasted from 112
to 105 BCE. Much of what survives about him comes through the Roman historian
Sallust. Again we meet a North African through the pen of the empire fighting
him. Jugurtha understood Rome well enough to know that its Senate could be
bought, its alliances divided, and its moral language used as a cover for
appetite. He fought the empire for years and was not captured cleanly in battle.
He was delivered through a political betrayal, taken to Rome, displayed in a
triumph that turned a defeated king into scenery for somebody else's glory, and
killed.

Rome preserved his name because Rome needed a defeated enemy large enough to
make its victory impressive. That is one of history's strange weaknesses. An
empire brags so loudly about crushing you that, centuries later, its brag becomes
proof that you once made it afraid.

A little more than a century after Jugurtha, Tacfarinas learned the Roman army
from the inside, deserted it, and turned that knowledge against it. From 17 to
24 CE he led the Musulamii and allied communities in [a war that forced
Rome](https://doi.org/10.1080/09592318.2020.1764708) to keep answering a North
African resistance it could not quickly extinguish. The Roman account calls him
deserter and bandit, because empires have always had a word that removes politics
from the violence of the people they rule. A Roman general fights for territory.
A North African fighting on North African land is a criminal disturbing the
peace. The description is already a verdict.

These men were not Moroccans in the modern national sense, just as Dihya did not
carry a modern flag. The borders had not been drawn and the identities we use
now did not yet exist in their present form. I am not interested in kidnapping
the ancient dead and issuing them twenty-first-century documents. I am interested
in the opposite: refusing to let modern borders make the old continuity
unthinkable. Numidia, Mauretania, the Aurès, the Rif, the Atlas, the Souss, the
desert routes between them—these were not empty waiting rooms between Europe and
the East. They were parts of a North African world whose people kept responding
to every empire that entered it.

And resistance is not the only history we lose when we forget that world. If we
remember Imazighen only while they are fighting invaders, then the invader still
controls the frame. A people does not exist only in the moment somebody attacks
it. Who did they become when they were building?

Look at Morocco's own medieval history. The Almoravids emerged from Sanhaja
Amazigh communities and made Marrakesh a capital. Under [Yusuf ibn
Tashfin](https://www.encyclopedia.com/people/history/north-african-history-biographies/yusuf-ibn-tashfin),
their state stretched across the western Maghreb and into al-Andalus. The Almohad
movement rose from the Masmuda of the High Atlas and built another enormous
North African empire. The Marinids who followed were Zenata Amazigh. The names
of the dynasties survive. Their monuments survive. Their victories enter the
schoolbook under the history of Morocco or the history of Islam. But the Amazigh
peoplehood of the human beings who built them is often softened into a tribal
footnote, as if naming it would make the achievement smaller.

It should make the story larger. These were Imazighen making Islamic civilization,
not standing outside it. They founded cities, organized states, crossed seas,
built mosques and schools, argued theology, fought each other, governed badly in
some years and brilliantly in others, exactly as historical peoples do. Islam
did not require them to stop being indigenous to North Africa. That false choice
came later, when Arabness was allowed to swallow everything Muslim and Amazighness
was pushed backward into the category of whatever existed before the faith.

That is another kind of whitening: not deleting the achievement, but changing
the identity attached to it. Keep Marrakesh; blur the people who founded it. Keep
the empire; reduce Sanhaja and Masmuda to pronunciation problems in a textbook.
Keep the architecture, the trade routes, the scholarship, and the state, then
tell the descendants that their ancestors lived outside history in tents until
history arrived speaking someone else's language.

Dihya was not the last Amazigh person to make history. She was one of the last
ones the simplified story finds impossible to rename.

## We were taught that dignity came from somewhere else

Ask people where they come from and watch how quickly geography becomes status.
Someone will reach east across a continent for a lineage whose proof is a family
story repeated long enough to become a document. Someone else will borrow an
ancestor from al-Andalus, or from a noble house, or from any distant place that
sounds grander than the village where every known grandparent was born. The
local root is treated like a failure of imagination. The farther the origin, the
more valuable it feels.

I do not think every person who calls himself Arab is lying. Arabness is a real
cultural identity. Language is real, faith is real, migration was real, and the
Maghreb has always mixed people because it sits where a continent meets a sea.
There are families with histories of arrival, and there are people for whom
Arab is the honest name of the culture that formed them. Identity is not a lab
result and no strand of DNA gets to overrule a life.

But there is another process we should be honest enough to name: Arabization is
not the same thing as the total replacement of a population. A person can inherit
an Arabic language and an Amazigh land, an Arab story and North African
ancestors. A population can change the name it gives itself much faster than it
changes its blood. [Genome-wide studies of North
Africa](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3257290/) find exactly that
complicated picture: deep, autochthonous Maghrebi ancestry alongside ancestry
from migrations through the Middle East, Europe, and sub-Saharan Africa. The
research does not reveal a pure people. No honest research ever does. It reveals
continuity underneath mixture.

The point is not that DNA proves every Moroccan is Amazigh. The point is that it
breaks the childish equation in which speaking Arabic means descending entirely
from Arabia. Genes do not speak. Conquests often move languages farther than
they move people. Many Moroccans who imagine the Amazigh as somebody else, a
minority living in a mountain at a safe distance, may be describing a branch of
their own family that changed languages and then forgot that changing languages
was the thing that happened.

Why is the distant ancestor so attractive? Because power assigned prestige to
distance. The conqueror's origin became noble; the native's origin became a
thing to escape. Generations later, a person repeats the prestigious genealogy
not because someone is forcing him, but because the ranking has moved inside
him. Colonialism no longer needs an officer at the door once it has installed a
customs agent in the mind.

## The mouth remembers after the person forgets

There is a beautiful problem with trying to erase a people: the body is a bad
liar.

Listen to Moroccan Darija next to the Arabic spoken farther east. The words are
often recognizably Arabic, but the mouth is doing something North African with
them. The vowels thin out. Consonants gather where another dialect would separate
them. The rhythm lands differently. Centuries of contact with Amazigh languages
have [left marks in Moroccan Arabic's sound system and word
formation](https://doi.org/10.13140/RG.2.2.12030.69447), as well as its vocabulary
and parts of its grammar. Linguists argue over the size and source of every
influence, as they should, but the contact itself is not seriously in doubt.
Darija is not Arabic badly pronounced. It is a Moroccan language shaped by the
full history of Moroccan mouths.

That makes it a kind of living archaeological site. An Arab word can enter an
Amazigh mouth, be carried by an Amazigh rhythm, settle into an Amazigh pattern,
and travel for centuries until the speaker no longer knows why it feels natural
there. The memory survives beneath the speaker's explanation of himself. He may
say the Amazigh have nothing to do with him while using a language whose very
breathing was changed by their tongues.

The same thing happens outside language. It survives in the architecture that
understands heat, in the grain stored for a bad season, in the tattoo remembered
after tattooing was shamed, in carpets whose symbols outlived the names of the
women who designed them, in communal land, wedding music, silver, clay, oil,
bread, and the stubborn knowledge of how to live in a difficult geography. A
culture is not a museum cabinet of pure objects. It is a set of solutions the
dead leave inside the habits of the living.

We live on top of those solutions. Sometimes literally. The paths follow old
paths. The villages sit beside older villages. The lessons of the ancestors are
under the foundations, and so are their graves. Then we call the culture ours
while treating the people who made it as strangers. We take the inheritance and
reject the name on the will.

## Souss carried its library in the throat

In the south, history did not wait for permission to enter a book. It learned to
travel inside a person.

The Souss has a word, [*amarg*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/review-of-middle-east-studies/article/from-the-heights-of-the-atlas-a-panorama-of-traditional-poetry-in-tamazight/DD2E315B6FCFA705136B860AF293ED60),
that refuses the small boxes another language tries to put around it. It can mean
poetry. It can mean song, love, wisdom, heartache, longing, the ache of being away
from home. The meanings belong together because the poem was never only a
decorated sentence. It was a way of carrying what absence does to a person, what
injustice does to a village, what love does to judgment, what migration does to
the meaning of home. The culture did not separate literature from music and then
store each in a different department. The thought was sung because singing gave
it legs.

That is what the *rrways* and *tarrwaysin*, the men and women poet-singers of the
Souss, carried from place to place. A rays was not merely an entertainer hired
to fill silence after dinner. The poet had to know language at its sharpest,
metaphor, religious learning, memory, rhythm, the temper of an audience, and the
exact pressure required to say a dangerous thing without flattening it into a
slogan. The troupe moved between villages and towns. The ribab, the voice, the
percussion, and the poem arrived together. Where the written newspaper did not
reach, the song could. Where an ordinary person could not confront power
directly, a verse could make the accusation memorable enough to survive the
night.

The old songs contain love, but calling them love songs in the modern sense is
too small. They contain migration and the wound of leaving Souss for the northern
cities or France. They contain drought, faith, work, betrayal, changing manners,
the price of things, the distance between rich and poor, the shock of technology,
and arguments about what the community was becoming. A song did what an essay
does, what a newspaper column does, what a sermon does, what a letter home does.
Then it did something none of those can do: it entered the memory of people who
could not read it and let them carry it intact enough to give away again.

Lhaj Belaid, born in the nineteenth century and still heard long after his death
in 1945, became one of the great names of that art. His recordings caught only a
piece of a tradition that had lived without recording machines. Mohamed Demsiri
carried it into another generation. Rkiya Talbensirt, Fatima Tihihit, and Fatima
Tabaamrant make another fact impossible to ignore: women were not silent material
inside this culture's poetry. They were authors, voices, interpreters, and public
memory. Tabaamrant could take the old form and make it speak directly about
Amazigh language and rights. Continuity did not mean repeating an artifact
without touching it. It meant giving an old instrument the courage to describe
a new wound.

Then there is ahwach, which an outsider can watch for ten minutes and file under
folk dance. That is what a culture looks like when you remove the intelligence
and keep the costume.

Ahwach is collective before it is spectacular. Lines or circles of bodies make
rhythm together. Drums organize the space. Voices answer voices. Men and women
can face one another across the performance, and poets can improvise in public,
answering a verse with another verse while everyone around them understands the
quality of the reply. A wedding becomes a literary arena. The community is not
quietly consuming a finished cultural product; it is testing language in real
time. Wit, memory, courage, timing, and command of Tachelhit become public forms
of intelligence.

This matters because the usual story of literacy teaches us to respect only the
knowledge that sits still. If it can be placed on a shelf, assigned an author,
and quoted in the language of the institution, it is literature. If it lives in
the coordination of fifty people, if its finest line was improvised once at a
wedding and then carried by whoever heard it, it becomes folklore. One is treated
as thought. The other is treated as atmosphere.

But oral does not mean thoughtless, and collective does not mean authorless in
the sense of coming from nobody. It means the archive had a different storage
system. A poem was kept redundantly across human memories. A rhythm helped with
error correction. Repetition preserved the line. Performance attached the words
to a night, a place, a marriage, a face, so remembering one could recover the
others. The neighborhood was the library, and every fluent elder held volumes
with no duplicate anywhere else.

That library is burning quietly now.

Not because the music has vanished completely. There are recordings, festivals,
new groups, fusions, videos, and young artists refusing the funeral. An
[anthology of one hundred songs](https://elpais.com/planeta-futuro/2021-02-26/un-viaje-de-cien-canciones-hacia-las-raices-de-los-pueblos-bereber.html)
brought together around eighty rrways and tarrwaysin precisely because people
understood how much remained at risk. The art is changing, as every living art
must. I do not want to preserve it under glass until no one is allowed to make a
new sound with it.

The danger is deeper than whether the melody remains available online. A song
can survive as audio and die as meaning. The child can recognize the chorus but
not the image inside it. He can dance to his grandparents' language while needing
subtitles to know what his grandparents said. The metaphor depends on a word no
longer used, a farming practice no longer seen, a proverb nobody completed for
him, and gradually the poem becomes a beautiful wall he cannot find the door
through.

When an old singer dies without being recorded, we do not lose one voice. We
lose every variation that voice knew, every occasion on which a line should be
used, every correction they would have made to the younger singer, every story
about who first sang it and why. When a language retreats, the music does not
lose only lyrics. It loses its system of references. Translation can carry the
meaning of the sentence, but not always the social world that made the sentence
land.

This is why a dying musical culture cannot be rescued only with a stage. The
stage may save the performance while the home loses the language that produced
it. A festival can applaud ahwach once a year while the school spends the rest
of the year teaching the child that serious thought happens elsewhere. The same
country can celebrate the costume and starve the conditions that make the next
poet possible.

## The south also wrote, and we forgot how to read it

Even the claim that Tachelhit lived only in the throat is incomplete. The Souss
also built [a manuscript
tradition](https://www.koeppe.de/titel_print_the-berber-literary-tradition-of-the-sous),
and forgetting it has allowed people to mistake marginalization for absence.

Known Tachelhit manuscripts reach back to around 1580. Scholars, copyists, and
poets used Arabic letters, adapted and vocalized to hold an Amazigh language.
That fact alone should break several of the false walls we inherited. The script
was Arabic; the language was Tachelhit; the subjects included religion, law,
advice, narrative, and poetry. These writers did not experience Islam and
Amazighness as mutually exclusive rooms. They used the tools available to make
their own language carry scholarship.

Muhammad Awzal, born in the Souss around 1680, stands inside this tradition. In
1711 he completed *Al-Hawd*, *The Reservoir*, a long religious work in versified
Tachelhit. He wrote *Bahr al-Dumu'*, *The Ocean of Tears*, and other works that
were copied again and again. Nearly a third of the known Tachelhit manuscript
corpus contains at least part of his writing. The image of Amazigh as a language
that could sing but could not think on paper collapses in front of a shelf that
was there the whole time.

And notice how complete the erasure has to be for this to surprise us. First, a
language is denied sustained institutional support. Then the manuscripts that do
exist become specialist objects, held in private collections or libraries far
from the descendants who might read them. Then the public is told that the lack
of a large visible written canon proves the language was never suited for one.
The wound is presented as anatomy. What was prevented becomes evidence that the
people were incapable.

Awzal matters to me for more than the number of surviving pages. He joins worlds
we are often told to pull apart. An Amazigh poet could write Islamic teaching in
Tachelhit using Arabic script. The language of the ancestors was not an obstacle
to faith. Faith was not proof of an Arab bloodline. A person could receive a
religion from the East, make it intimate in the language of the Souss, and
produce something neither world would have made alone.

That is what a living culture does. It does not protect itself by refusing every
arrival. It takes what arrives, argues with it, translates it, changes its sound,
and makes it answer local questions. The proof of continuity is not purity. It
is the ability to transform without disappearing.

So when I say the poetry is dying, I do not mean there was once a fixed golden
age and every new guitar is a betrayal. I mean the chain of deep comprehension
is thinning. Fewer people can inherit the poem without translation. Fewer know
the references that make one line open into ten meanings. Fewer can move between
the manuscript, the improvised reply, the wedding chorus, and the ordinary
speech of an elder and recognize them as rooms in the same house.

Saving the music means recording, yes. It means archives, editions, translations,
and paying artists enough to remain artists. But it also means children learning
Tachelhit well enough to make new jokes in it, new science in it, new love poems
and bad songs and political arguments in it. A language survives not when every
old song has been preserved, but when a young person can create a song the
ancestors never heard and still be answered by them through the form.

## Before school, the world spoke Amazigh

I know this inheritance first as a feeling because I was born inside it, in the
south of Morocco near Agadir. Amazigh was not a political position in my
childhood. It was not a box on a census form, a paragraph in the Constitution,
or an argument about who arrived first. It was simply the temperature of the
room. People around me spoke it. I heard it before I knew that a language could
be ranked, before I knew that one tongue could be called official and another
could be left outside the classroom. It was how the world reached me while the
world still felt whole.

The culture was everywhere because nobody had separated it from ordinary life
yet. It was in weddings that seemed to gather more people than any one family
could contain. It was in the singing, the call answered by another voice, the
music carrying across the night, the dancing that did not need to be explained
because your body learned the meaning before your mind asked for one. You did
not attend the culture. You stood inside it. There was no stage on one side and
an audience on the other. The person singing, the person dancing, the aunt
preparing food, the neighbor moving chairs, and the child falling asleep in the
middle of all of it were making the same thing together.

And you belonged to more people than the ones in your house. The whole
neighborhood participated in raising you, sometimes gently, sometimes with the
authority of people who had never asked your parents for permission because care
itself was the permission. If you were hungry, somebody fed you. If you were
somewhere you should not be, somebody corrected you. If something happened to
your family, the walls between households became temporarily imaginary. I did
not have language for communal care then because children do not name the air.
They only notice it when they leave and breathing becomes harder.

Nothing about this felt like heritage. Heritage is what adults call a living
thing after they become afraid it might die. To us it was natural. The songs did
not know they were preserving history. The wedding did not announce that it was
performing Amazigh identity. The neighbors did not call themselves a social
safety net. People were just living according to lessons old enough that nobody
remembered who taught the first one.

Then we went to school, and the world changed languages.

## The first lesson was translation

The classroom spoke Arabic. We could speak Arabic, yes, and fluently at that.
This was not the clean story of a child understanding nothing while a teacher
spoke over his head. We understood the sentences. We could answer the questions,
read the page, memorize the line, and produce it again on the exam. From the
outside, the system worked. A fluent child had been taught by a fluent teacher
in an official language, and the marks on the paper could prove it.

But I could speak Arabic without feeling the world through it.

That difference is difficult to explain to someone who learned in the same
language that first named things for them. A first language does more than attach
labels to objects. It contains the web between them. A word arrives with the
memory of who used it, the face they made, the situation that called for it, the
other words that live nearby. It is not a definition. It is a small world. In a
second language, you can know the definition and still be standing outside the
world.

So there were holes everywhere, small enough to be invisible to the teacher and
large enough for understanding to fall through. I would meet a word I did not
know and guess its meaning from the sentence, then build the next idea on top of
that guess. Years later I would learn that the word did not mean what I had
thought it meant. It meant something adjacent, or something narrower, or the
opposite under a certain condition, and suddenly I could see how much knowledge
I had stacked on a crooked first brick. The mistake was not always large enough
to fail an exam. That almost made it worse. The exam moved on, so the hole moved
with me.

This is what gets hidden by the word *fluent*. Fluency tells you that a student
can move through the language. It does not tell you what he loses while moving.
It does not tell you how much of his attention is being spent translating the
question before he can think about the answer. It does not count the ideas he
never reaches because all his working memory was used crossing the bridge to
them. A child learning mathematics should be wrestling with mathematics. A child
learning science should be wrestling with the universe. Instead, part of us was
always wrestling with the language in which the universe had been assigned.

And Arabic was only the second language if we simplify the situation. Between
the Amazigh of home and the Arabic of the textbook stood Darija, the language of
the street, itself carrying Arabic words through a deeply Moroccan mouth. We
call it Arabic when that is administratively convenient, but put me in front of
a man from Jordan and watch how little of my ordinary speech reaches him. I am
supposedly speaking his language, yet he understands almost nothing I say. The
distance is not an accent sitting politely on top. It is vocabulary, rhythm,
sound, structure, history. Calling both things Arabic does not make the distance
disappear; it only makes the child responsible for pretending it is not there.

So count the crossings. Home gives you the first map of reality in Amazigh.
Daily life moves through Darija. School opens the book in standard Arabic. Later,
French arrives carrying science, administration, medicine, and status, and
English may arrive after that carrying the internet and research. Each new
language can become a gift; I would not trade away the worlds they opened. But
the gift has a cost when it is used as a gate. Before a child can add knowledge
to the civilization, he has to prove he can reach the language where the
knowledge has been stored.

That arrangement limits more than one student. It limits a whole population's
ability to build knowledge from the ground it already stands on. The child who
could ask a precise, alive question in his first language learns to offer a
smaller question in the classroom language, the one he can assemble without
risk. The parent who understands the land, weather, animals, birth, repair, or
human nature in extraordinary detail cannot easily enter the written system
where expertise is certified. Knowledge remains rich in one language and
powerful in another, and instead of building a bridge wide enough for both, we
mistake the traffic jam for a lack of intelligence.

How much knowledge did Morocco fail to build because the person who carried the
insight did not carry the approved words? How many children learned that being
smart meant translating themselves successfully? How many eventually became
excellent at the translation and forgot there had once been something on the
other side worth bringing across?

This is where language policy becomes memory policy. When a school refuses to
let a child begin from his first language, it does not merely choose a medium of
instruction. It quietly tells him where knowledge is allowed to live. Arabic is
where serious things can be written. French is where advanced things can be
studied. Amazigh is where the grandmother speaks, where the wedding sings, where
the joke works, where the past sits. One language gets the future. The other is
assigned affection.

A language cannot survive on affection alone.

## Official on paper, fragile in the hand

Morocco's Constitution has called Amazigh an official language since 2011 and a
common heritage of all Moroccans without exception. The [organic law that
followed in 2019](https://www.sgg.gov.ma/BO/bo_fr/2021/BO_7000_Fr.pdf) laid out
its gradual integration into education and public life. These are not meaningless
changes. Tifinagh appears on public buildings and road signs. Tamazight exists in
broadcasting, classrooms, institutions, and law in a way it was once denied.
People fought for those lines, and the lines matter.

But a language can be official above a ministry door and still be disappearing
inside a home.

The [2024 national census](https://m.hcp.ma/file/242663/) says 24.8 percent of
Moroccans use an Amazigh language: Tachelhit, Tamazight, or Tarifit. Yet among
literate people aged ten and older, only 1.5 percent report being able to read
and write Amazigh in Tifinagh. Nearly everyone in that group can read and write
Arabic. More people can read and write French than can write the language the
Constitution calls the common heritage of every Moroccan.

There is the current state of the Amazigh question in one contradiction. The
language has climbed into the Constitution but has not yet climbed into the hand.
It is visible on the wall and vulnerable in the mouth. It is celebrated as
heritage, which is sometimes the polite stage before a living thing is turned
into decoration. A government can preserve the alphabet on a sign while the
last fluent conversation in a family happens between two grandparents.

And the numbers only count use, not inheritance. They cannot count the person
whose parents stopped speaking Tarifit to him because Arabic would help at
school. They cannot count the child who understands Tachelhit but answers in
Darija. They cannot count the family that changed its name, the shame attached
to an accent, or the sentence spoken in a classroom that convinced a student
his mother's language belonged outside. Language loss rarely feels like a
historical event while it is happening. It feels like a practical decision made
one child at a time.

That is why legal recognition, necessary as it is, cannot repair this alone.
The wound is not only administrative. It is intimate. You can order an office
to add Tifinagh. You cannot order a person to stop feeling embarrassed when his
grandmother speaks.

## The worst thing colonialism can do

The ordinary list of colonial crimes is already long: land taken, labor
extracted, people killed, wealth redirected, borders drawn for somebody else's
convenience. These are visible crimes. They leave ruins, ledgers, bones. They can
be named because the wound and the weapon are still in the same picture.

But the worst thing colonialism can do is make the wound defend the weapon.

It can wash the color from a people's consciousness so thoroughly that they
become afraid of the thing that makes them human: memory. Not memory as nostalgia,
not a costume worn at a festival, but the accumulated past lives inside the
present one. The knowledge that I am not self-created. That my language was
shaped by mouths I will never meet. That my instincts about family, land,
hospitality, dignity, and survival were rehearsed by people whose names did not
make it into the official book. That the dead are not gone; they are load-bearing.

Take that knowledge from a person and you do more than alter his history. You
make him lonely in time. He begins to think he appeared recently, from nowhere,
owing nothing to the people beneath him. He can be sold any origin after that,
especially one that flatters him, because a person cut off from memory will buy
roots the way a thirsty person buys water.

This is why forgetting is not neutral. Forgetting serves the last narrator.
Every blank space becomes available for his version. Every ancestor whose name
we are ashamed to say becomes room for a conqueror to place his own portrait.
The whitening does not end when the foreign flag comes down. It ends when the
people can look backward without needing the past to resemble the power that
defeated it.

## Memory without a purity myth

Remembering cannot mean inventing a clean Amazigh past to replace an invented
clean Arab one. North Africa was never sealed. People crossed it, traded through
it, married into it, prayed differently inside it, changed languages, and made
new things from every encounter. Purity is another conqueror's idea, useful for
deciding who belongs and who does not. I do not want it, even when it is offered
in my favor.

What I want is continuity without shame.

I want a Moroccan to be able to say Arab without needing the Amazigh to vanish,
and Amazigh without being treated as less Moroccan, less Muslim, less modern, or
less educated. I want Darija understood not as damaged Arabic but as evidence of
what this land does to every language it adopts: it makes it answer to the mouths
already here. I want Dihya remembered neither as a flawless saint nor as the
superstitious villain left to us by later chronicles, but as proof that the
people of this land had names, leaders, arguments, and a history before someone
else arrived to write the chapter. I want Jugurtha and Tacfarinas recovered from
the Roman victory parade without pretending they were simple men fighting simple
wars. I want the Almoravids and Almohads remembered as Amazigh makers of Islamic
civilization, because neither half of that sentence cancels the other.

I want Muhammad Awzal returned from the specialist shelf to the language that
made him. I want the rrways and tarrwaysin treated as thinkers, not background
music. I want a child in the Souss to hear an old line of amarg and receive more
than its melody, to understand the image, the argument, the ache inside the word,
and then feel free to answer it with a line nobody has written yet. Preservation
should not mean teaching the young to imitate the dead. It should mean giving
them enough memory to disagree with the dead in the same language.

Most of all, I want us to stop treating our ancestors like an accusation.

The point of recovering a root is not to become trapped beneath it. A root is
not a command to live backward. It is what lets a living thing grow without
falling over. Morocco does not need less of the centuries that passed through
it. It needs the courage to admit what those centuries passed through: an
existing people, still here, mixed but continuous, speaking sometimes in
Tamazight and sometimes through the Amazigh memory hidden inside Darija,
carrying old solutions in new lives.

The winner wrote the first version. He named the queen, paraded the king, called
the rebel a bandit, reduced the builders to tribes, the poets to folklore, the
language to a sign above a ministry door. He made distance noble and the local
embarrassing. But he does not get the last version unless we keep repeating it
for him.

The graves are still under our feet. Awzal's words are still on the page. The
old songs are still moving through weddings, taxis, phones, and voices that have
not gone quiet yet. The language is still inside our mouths. The memory is not
gone. It is waiting for us to stop being afraid of recognizing ourselves in it,
and to answer before another library disappears.
