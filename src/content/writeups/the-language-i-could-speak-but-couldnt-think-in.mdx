For years, some of the words in my head meant the wrong thing.

Not dramatically wrong. That would have been easier. If I had thought *water*
meant *fire*, somebody would have corrected me before I could build anything on
top of it. The dangerous mistakes were smaller. A word meant something close to
what I thought it meant, close enough to survive a classroom, an exam, sometimes
several years. I could place it in a sentence. I could recognize it on a page. I
could repeat the definition the teacher had given us and receive the mark that
said I understood.

Then, much later, I would meet the word again in a context where the old meaning
could not hold, and I would discover that it was narrower than I had thought, or
wider, or that it carried a distinction my first understanding had erased. The
correction did not repair one word. It opened a hole beneath every idea I had
built using it. I could suddenly see the crooked first brick and the years of
wall balanced above it.

Nobody had noticed the hole, including me, because I spoke Arabic fluently.

That sentence is the whole problem. I could speak the language, read it, write
it, answer questions in it, and pass through an education conducted in it. By
every measurement available to the school, the language had reached me. What
the measurements could not see was that I had not reached the world through the
language.

In [the last writeup](/blog/writeups/the-ancestors-we-were-taught-to-forget/),
I wrote about the final victory of conquest: teaching a people to forget their
ancestors. This is the same argument seen from inside one child's head. A history
can be removed from a schoolbook in a year. Removing the language through which
the child might have questioned that book takes longer. It happens one lesson at
a time.

I could speak Arabic. I could not think in it without first crossing a distance.

## Before a child has vocabulary, he has a world

I was born in the south of Morocco, near Agadir, and the first world I knew spoke
both Tachelhit and Darija. I did not meet either of them as a subject. I learned
them from the street: from family and neighbors, from games, arguments, errands,
weddings, jokes, warnings, and all the ordinary exchanges through which a child
slowly discovers what kind of world he has entered.

I do not mean only that people around me used Amazigh words for objects. A first
language is not a box of labels a child sticks onto a finished reality. The
language participates in making the reality. It tells him which differences
matter enough to receive different names. It teaches him what can be left
unsaid, what tone can carry, what a pause means, which joke becomes funnier when
the sentence ends early because everyone already knows the missing word.

Tachelhit and Darija arrived attached to faces, places, and consequences. A word
for care came with the person who cared for me. A word for danger came with the
change in an adult's voice that meant I should stop before I understood why.
Respect was not a definition. It was a map of how to stand near an elder, how to
answer, when not to answer, and how the same silence could mean obedience in one
moment and hurt in another. These languages entered through an entire body
before either of them ever entered through text.

The neighborhood spoke them. The weddings spoke them. The women preparing food,
the men moving chairs, the children disappearing into a crowd of relatives and
neighbors who all had temporary authority over them, all of it spoke through
them. Music did not translate itself before reaching me. The rhythm, the line of poetry, the
reply from the other side of the ahwach, the laughter that arrived before I was
old enough to understand every layer of the joke—these things taught me that
meaning could be shared before it was explained.

This is the first education every child receives, and it is the most successful
one. Nobody gives him a grammar lesson, yet he discovers the grammar. Nobody
tests his vocabulary, yet he adds to it every day. He learns intention, causality,
social rank, danger, love, deception, humor, time, and the weather through a
language nobody has yet told him is unsuitable for knowledge.

Then he arrives at school, and the institution behaves as if his mind has arrived
empty.

## The door of the school was a border

The classroom spoke Arabic.

We could speak Arabic. This needs to be repeated because the obvious objection
to this story is that perhaps we simply did not know the language. We knew it.
We were not sitting in total incomprehension while a teacher produced foreign
sounds. Arabic was on television, in religion, in public life, and around us in
different forms. We could form sentences. We could follow instructions. We
could become very good students in it.

But the Arabic of school was not the Tachelhit through which the world first
became coherent, and it was not quite the Darija through which ordinary Moroccan
life moved either. The language of the textbook arrived already standing above
us. It carried correctness. It carried the teacher, the answer key, the exam,
the government, the newsreader, the sermon, and the red pen. We did not meet it
as another way to say what we knew. We met it as the place where valid knowledge
lived.

The first lesson was therefore not mathematics or reading. The first lesson was
a hierarchy of minds.

At home, Darija could tell me that somebody was hungry, angry, generous,
ashamed, ill, or lying. It could carry a wedding and settle an argument. But the
school quietly assigned it a boundary. It was good enough to live in and not
good enough to learn through. Formal Arabic was where an idea became serious. A fact
said in Darija belonged to the family. The same fact printed in Arabic
belonged to knowledge.

No teacher had to say this aloud. Institutions teach most powerfully through
arrangement. Put one language in the mouth of the child and another in every
book, every examination, every certificate, every path out of poverty, and the
child will understand the ranking. He may never develop the vocabulary to
describe what happened. He will still learn which part of himself must wait
outside.

So I crossed the border every morning.

## Fluency is a terrible witness

The strange thing about language difficulty is that the better you become at
hiding it, the less help you receive.

A beginner is visible. He stops, asks, mispronounces, produces the kind of error
that interrupts the room. Everyone can see that language stands between him and
the lesson. A fluent student is more complicated. He has learned how to keep the
room moving. When he does not fully know a word, he infers from context. When a
sentence is dense, he holds the beginning in memory until the end makes it
probable. When he cannot express the exact question in his head, he asks the
smaller question he can build safely. When none of this works, he stays quiet.

The silence looks like understanding.

Fluency measures movement. It tells you that a person can travel through a
language at a useful speed. It says almost nothing about what the journey costs
him. Two students can give the same correct answer while one of them traveled
ten meters and the other crossed a border, translated the signposts, checked
whether the translation was socially acceptable, and arrived with no breath
left to wonder why the answer was true.

That was the part nobody could see: a fraction of the mind was always occupied
by the crossing.

When the teacher asked a question, the work began before the subject began. What
does this particular word mean here? Is it the word I know from religion, or
does science use it differently? What is the Darija equivalent? Is there a
Tachelhit idea nearby that makes this concrete? Can I answer with the words I
have, or will my language make a correct thought sound childish? By the time the
question reached the part of me capable of solving it, another student was
already raising a hand.

Speed is easily mistaken for intelligence in a classroom. The student whose
language sits closest to the language of instruction appears to understand
faster because he is doing less work before understanding can begin. The student
translating invisibly appears slower because only the final stage of his labor
is graded. The school times both runners from the same starting gun and refuses
to notice that one began a kilometer behind it.

This does not mean every difficulty I had was linguistic. That would be another
flattering lie. Sometimes I did not study enough. Sometimes the idea was hard.
Sometimes I was wrong in every language available to me. But the existence of
ordinary failure does not erase the additional tax. It only makes the tax harder
to isolate, because every bad result can be blamed on the child before anyone
asks how much of the examination was secretly an examination in translation.

## A definition can pass an exam without entering a mind

Schools are very good at rewarding the appearance of transfer.

The teacher gives a definition. The student copies it. The student memorizes the
sequence, reproduces it under pressure, and receives a mark. The words have moved
from the board to the notebook to the answer sheet. Because the words moved, we
say knowledge moved with them.

But knowledge is not the sentence. Knowledge is what the sentence connects to.

If I learn the definition of erosion but cannot attach it to the land I have
seen change after rain, I own a password, not a concept. If I can state a rule
of biology but cannot use it to reorganize the living things already present in
my experience, then the rule is stored as text waiting for the exam that will
retrieve it. It has not joined the rest of my mind. It cannot easily combine
with anything. It cannot surprise me.

A first language is powerful partly because it contains so many existing hooks.
New knowledge has somewhere to attach. A scientific idea can find the childhood
observation it explains, the tool a parent used, the behavior of an animal, the
season when the field changed, the household rule that suddenly reveals a hidden
logic. The child does not need to create the world and learn the idea at the
same time. The world is already there, dense with associations, waiting for a
better explanation.

In a less intimate language, the same definition can float. Every individual
word is known, but the sentence has no roots. You can carry it for years because
the school keeps asking for the same sentence back. Then someone asks you to use
the idea somewhere unfamiliar and it collapses. Memorization was not a moral
failure. It was the most rational response to a system in which words were easier
to transport than meaning.

This is how a student can be fluent, hardworking, successful, and full of holes.
The transcript records the floors he reached. It does not record the missing
stairs inside them.

## The language we borrow when we are ashamed

The distance did not exist only in school. It entered the most private parts of
life too.

There is a habit I began to notice around subjects considered *7chouma*: sex,
love, desire, the body, and sometimes even ordinary emotion. People who would
normally speak Darija or Tachelhit would suddenly reach for Arabic to explain
them. The language became more formal at the exact moment the subject became
more intimate. It was as if a borrowed register could place a clean sheet
between the speaker and what was being said.

This does not mean Arabic is incapable of intimacy. It means we often used it
as distance. A word learned from a textbook, a sermon, television, or an expert
could be pronounced without feeling as exposed as the equivalent that belonged
to the home. Formal language made the subject sound educational, medical, or
moral. It allowed us to discuss the human body while pretending that no human
body was present.

But the protection has a cost. When the language used to explain an experience
is separated from the language in which the experience is actually felt, the
explanation can remain outside the person. A young person may learn the approved
term for sex without learning how to speak about consent, fear, tenderness, or
confusion. He may know a grand word for love and still be unable to tell someone
close to him that he is lonely. He may recognize the name of an emotion on a
page while lacking words he can use when that emotion enters the room.

Then silence grows in the space between vocabulary and life. What cannot be
said naturally cannot be questioned naturally. What cannot be questioned is
left to rumor, shame, jokes, warnings, and whatever the street is willing to
teach without responsibility. We think we have protected people from the taboo,
but often we have protected the taboo from understanding.

This is another way a language hierarchy enters the body. One tongue carries
the life itself; another is summoned to make that life respectable enough to
mention. Between them stands the person, able to repeat an explanation yet
unable to place himself inside it.

## The teacher translated too

There was another clue that the official arrangement was not working: the
teacher often had to leave it in order to teach.

A difficult idea would be presented in formal Arabic. Faces would remain still.
The teacher would try again, perhaps more slowly, using nearly the same words.
Then, at some point, the language would loosen. Darija would enter. In an Amazigh
area, Tachelhit might enter if the teacher shared it. A familiar example would
appear. The room would change. Shoulders relaxed. Someone laughed. Hands rose.
The concept had not become simpler. It had become connected.

Then, before we wrote anything down, the idea had to change clothes again.

The explanation that made us understand was often not the language permitted in
the answer. We could discover the concept in Darija or Tachelhit, but we had to
return it to Arabic for certification. The living bridge was treated as a
temporary classroom trick. The official language remained the only one allowed
to appear in the record, so the record showed a system succeeding in Arabic
after the teacher had quietly rescued it through another tongue.

This produces a peculiar dishonesty. Everyone in the room knows that the lesson
needed translation. The teacher knows. The students know. The examiner may know
because the examiner once sat in a similar room. But the institution records
only the final Arabic, and therefore never has to admit how much understanding
was built outside it.

Darija performs this rescue across Morocco and is then dismissed as informal,
broken, merely spoken, not a real language. Tachelhit performs it where teachers
and students share the language and then disappears completely from the answer
sheet. The languages doing the cognitive labor receive none of the institutional
credit.

## Then science changed languages again

Just as Arabic became familiar enough to carry more of the load, another border
appeared.

French did not arrive merely as a subject. It arrived attached to science,
medicine, engineering, administration, higher education, employment, and the
class of people who seemed to move easily through all of them. The exact point
of transition has shifted across Moroccan reforms and generations, but the
underlying experience is familiar: concepts learned under one vocabulary later
reappear under another, and the student is expected to treat the switch as if
only the labels changed.

Only the labels did not simply change. The whole map changed with them.

Mathematics is often described as a universal language, which is true only after
you have reached the mathematics. The explanation surrounding the equation is
not universal. The word problem, the instruction, the distinction between prove,
calculate, infer, approximate, and justify, the sentence that tells you what the
symbols are doing—these belong to a human language, and misunderstanding one can
make you look incapable of the universal thing beneath it.

Science became a test of how rapidly we could rebuild the same conceptual house
under French names. Some students had been living near that language for years,
through private schooling, family, media, or class position. Others encountered
it mainly as schoolwork. Again the runners lined up at a painted line that hid
how far each had already traveled. Again linguistic proximity appeared on paper
as intelligence.

Morocco has spent decades moving instructional authority between Arabic and
French, particularly in science, while Amazigh and Darija remain largely outside
the formal contest. The debate usually asks which prestigious language should
own the advanced subject. It asks much less often what happens to the child who
must repeatedly rebuild his understanding as the ownership changes.

Later, English opened another world for me: research, computing, papers, people,
and ideas I might never have reached otherwise. I love what additional languages
gave me. I am writing this in English. That fact should prevent an easy conclusion
in which the answer is to close every door except the first one.

The problem was never that I learned too many languages. The problem was that I
had to abandon the ground each time I entered a new one.

## Multilingualism and repeated displacement are not the same thing

People defend this system by saying languages are an advantage, and they are
right. A new language is not only a new vocabulary. It is access to another
archive, another humor, another set of intuitions, another population of minds.
Arabic connected me to religion, literature, history, and hundreds of millions
of people. French opened institutions and texts that Morocco had placed behind
it. English opened much of the scientific and technical present. I would not
give any of them back.

But multilingualism should mean accumulation. What we often practiced was
displacement.

The distinction is simple. In an additive system, the child begins from the
language in which he understands most deeply, learns through it, develops
literacy and conceptual confidence, and adds other languages as bridges to more
worlds. Each language increases his reach. In a subtractive system, every new
language becomes evidence that the previous one should retreat. The child does
not add a tool. He changes the part of himself allowed to be intelligent.

We congratulate the final multilingual adult and ignore how many children were
lost during the crossings.

The survivor makes the system look generous. Look, he speaks four languages.
Look how adaptable Moroccans are. What the survivor cannot show us is the classmate
who understood the subject but could not make the linguistic transition quickly
enough, the student who decided science was not for her when French was the real
barrier, the child who concluded he was stupid because the only intelligence the
school could hear arrived in a language he did not yet command.

Adaptability is admirable. Requiring it before a child is permitted to learn is
not.

## The language ladder is also a class ladder

Languages in Morocco are not arranged only by function. They are arranged by
power.

An accent can reveal where someone grew up, what kind of school their parents
could afford, which rooms they have already been trained to enter, and whether
they expect the person across the desk to accommodate them. A French sentence
pronounced with ease can be treated as evidence of competence before its content
has been examined. Tachelhit in the wrong room can make the same mind appear
provincial before it has said anything at all.

This is why telling children simply to work harder at languages misses the
structure. Some children receive the language of later opportunity early, in
small classrooms, through books, conversation, travel, and parents who can
correct them without humiliation. Others first encounter it as the condition
for understanding a subject they are already being graded on. One child learns
French and then learns science. Another is expected to do both simultaneously,
and the resulting gap is recorded under science.

The language can then launder inequality. The final selection appears neutral:
everyone sat the same examination. But sameness at the end does not correct
unequal preparation at the beginning. It hides it beautifully.

And because linguistic prestige is audible, the hierarchy reproduces itself in
ordinary life. Parents who want to protect their children begin speaking the
language of mobility at home, even imperfectly. Tachelhit becomes something the
grandparents use with each other. The child understands but answers in Darija.
Then the next child understands less. Nobody decided to kill the language. Each
family made a practical decision under a system that had already assigned the
prices.

Language loss rarely arrives looking like hatred. It arrives looking like good
parenting.

## What cannot be said cannot join the institution

The deepest cost is not what the student fails to receive. It is what the
country fails to hear from him.

Knowledge does not flow in only one direction, from textbook to child. Every
child arrives carrying observations the institution does not yet have. Every
community contains expertise formed outside formal education: about land, water,
plants, animals, birth, construction, conflict, trade, care, weather, memory,
and the local forms of a problem that a national policy can see only from far
away.

But for an observation to become recognized knowledge, it must cross into the
approved language. The farmer may understand the soil and not possess the French
in which an agronomist will publish. The grandmother may understand human
behavior with a precision no questionnaire can reproduce and not possess the
Arabic in which her insight would be considered quotable. A child may notice a
pattern and lack the classroom vocabulary to make the pattern sound intelligent.

We call the failure to cross a lack of knowledge.

This reverses reality. The knowledge existed. The institution lacked the ears
for it.

Imagine what it does to a civilization when millions of people must translate
an insight before they are allowed to contribute it. Translation itself is not
the enemy; human knowledge depends on it. The danger is making translation the
price of admission and then confusing failure to pay with having nothing to
offer. Every border loses travelers. Every linguistic border around knowledge
loses ideas.

Over generations, the loss changes what people attempt. If Tachelhit is never
where mathematics happens, nobody develops the mathematical register it needs.
Then the absence of vocabulary is cited as proof that mathematics cannot happen
there. If the language is not used for scientific writing, scientific terms do
not become ordinary. Then its lack of ordinary scientific terms is treated as
a natural limitation rather than the predictable result of institutional
neglect.

A language is accused of being unable to carry the weight it has never been
allowed to lift.

## We were allowed to feel in it

This is the arrangement that troubles me most.

Tachelhit was not completely forbidden. A completely forbidden language creates
resistance because the violence is visible. Ours was allowed to remain in the
places power considered harmless. It could carry affection. It could carry a
joke, a wedding, an old song, a conversation with a grandmother, a memory of the
village. It could decorate a festival and appear beneath Arabic on a public
sign. It could be heritage.

It was simply denied the future tense.

When a language is allowed to hold only the past, even love can become part of
its burial. We speak warmly about preserving it while making every practical
decision in favor of something else. We record the old singer but do not create
the conditions for the next poet to speak about artificial intelligence,
climate change, loneliness in a modern city, or a scientific discovery in the
same language. We teach the child that Tachelhit is who his ancestors were and
leave him no image of what he might become through it.

Affection without authority turns a language into an heirloom. You handle it
carefully, show it to visitors, and never use it to build anything.

I do not want Tachelhit saved only because it is old. Age is not its strongest
claim. I want it alive because there are thoughts people can form more honestly
inside it now. Because a child should not have to distance himself from his
first mind before he is allowed to develop a second one. Because the language
that can carry grief and humor can also carry a theorem if we invest in the
words, teachers, books, and patience required. Every technical language was
once considered incapable of technical thought before people chose to do the
work in it.

## Beginning from home does not mean staying there

The answer is not to place an Amazigh child inside one language and lock the
door.

He should learn Arabic well. He should learn French if it remains useful to the
world he must navigate. He should learn English and any other language that
expands what he can read, make, and become. A child near Agadir deserves the
Quran, Arabic poetry, Francophone literature, global science, and the internet.
He should inherit more worlds than I did, not fewer.

But he should enter them from somewhere.

Education can begin with the language in which the child's concepts are densest
and build deliberate bridges outward. Teach literacy in the home language while
introducing the national and international languages systematically. Let the
teacher move between languages openly rather than pretending the translation
is a failure of discipline. Build bilingual terminology. Allow a student to
show the concept before linguistic polish becomes the next objective. Train
teachers who can teach across the actual languages in the room, not the imaginary
monolingual classroom described by policy.

This is not a sentimental theory. [UNESCO's guidance on multilingual
education](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/new-unesco-guidance-calls-multilingual-education-unlock-learning-and-inclusion)
recommends beginning learning in the language children understand best and
adding further languages through trained teachers and context-specific bridges.
The principle is almost embarrassingly obvious: understanding is the foundation
on which additional languages should be built, not the prize awarded after a
child survives them.

Morocco's difficulty is real. Tachelhit is not the only Amazigh variety. Darija
itself varies. Teachers, materials, terminology, scripts, mobility between
regions, and the need for shared national languages make implementation hard.
There is no switch that produces a perfectly multilingual education system on
Monday morning.

But difficulty is not an argument for preserving the current damage. It is an
argument for beginning the work honestly. We have spent generations asking
children to solve the complexity privately inside their heads. The system saves
itself the inconvenience and sends the entire cognitive bill to a six-year-old.

## The words I learned too late

I sometimes wonder which version of me would exist if knowledge had first been
allowed to meet me where I was.

Not a version who spoke only Tachelhit. Not a version protected from every
difficult word. Difficulty is not the wound, and struggle is part of learning.
I mean a version who could spend the struggle on the idea itself. A boy allowed
to wrestle with mathematics instead of mathematics plus Arabic, then science
plus French, then research plus English. A boy whose first language was treated
as cognitive capital rather than baggage he would eventually learn to hide.

Perhaps the result would not be dramatically different. I made it through. I
learned the languages. I crossed the borders and built a life in fields my
childhood could not name. It is tempting to use that survival as proof that no
harm was done.

But survival is a poor measurement of a system. It tells you who arrived, not
what they had to leave behind or who disappeared from the road.

I can write in English now. I can think in it, though not in exactly the same
way I think in the languages beneath it. Arabic no longer feels like the same
wall it once did. French became a tool. Darija remains the fast, improvised
country between formal worlds. Tachelhit is still the place where some meanings
arrive before their explanation.

The languages do not sit in separate rooms anymore. They interrupt one another,
lend one another words, reveal gaps, and sometimes rescue a thought the others
could not complete. This is what multilingualism can become when it stops being
a sequence of expulsions: not four incomplete selves, but one mind with more
than one way home.

What I mourn is that the system made this integration an individual accident.
It did not help us build it. It tested whether we could build it alone.

And I keep returning to those words I understood incorrectly for years. I do
not remember the first one. That feels appropriate. The injury was never one
word large enough to preserve as a story. It was a thousand almost-correct
meanings, each too small to trigger an alarm, together shaping the borders of
what I could understand and what I believed I was capable of understanding.

They let us speak Arabic and called us fluent. They let us reproduce knowledge
and called us educated. They never asked whether the language had become a place
where knowledge could live inside us.

I could speak it. I could pass through it. I could make myself legible to every
institution that required it.

But before I learned to think in it, I first had to learn that the language I
already thought in was not allowed to count.
