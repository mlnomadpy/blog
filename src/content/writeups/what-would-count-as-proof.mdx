I have a bachelor's degree and a master's in mathematics. Before those, there
was the high-school diploma. I can list the qualifications without hesitation.

I have more trouble saying that I am a mathematician.

There are too many things I still do not understand. Subjects I could study
for years. Arguments I cannot yet follow. Questions for which I would have to
begin much earlier than I feel someone with my education should need to begin.
The qualifications do not disappear when I think about those things. They
simply stop feeling like an answer.

I know I studied mathematics. I know I earned the degrees. What I cannot quite
settle is how much knowledge the word *mathematician* asks me to possess before
I can use it without feeling that I have overstated something.

The same hesitation follows me into AI, software engineering, and electrical
engineering. I have
evidence that I can do the work. Between me and myself, the doubt remains.

Nobody needs to accuse me. Nobody even needs to be there.

So what would I need to have? Another qualification? A particular result? The
ability to answer a question I cannot answer now?

What would count as proof, and would I accept it if I had it?

## What I can say the diploma proves

Perhaps part of the difficulty is that I do not connect my diplomas very
strongly with the knowledge I have.

I do not mean that the degrees were meaningless. They took study and work.
They record something I completed. But when I ask myself whether I understand
mathematics, I am asking about what I can do with an idea now. Can I explain
why an argument holds? Can I reconstruct the reasoning? Can I recognize when
an assumption matters instead of merely remembering that it was on the page?

A qualification cannot answer each of those questions for me.

There is a reasonable concern here, and I do not want reassurance to erase it.
Passing an assessment and understanding a subject deeply are not identical.
Knowing a result and knowing how to arrive at it are different achievements.
There are gaps worth finding, including gaps in things I have already studied.
If a piece of work depends on one of them, the degree does not do the missing
work on my behalf.

But there is another step I seem to take almost immediately.

I find something I do not understand, and the question becomes whether I
deserve the name at all.

That is much more than the discovery tells me. The gap might be serious. It
might limit what I can responsibly attempt or explain. It does not, by itself,
tell me what to make of everything I do understand. Yet the missing knowledge
feels more revealing than the knowledge I have. It seems to show the truth
about me, while the qualifications remain something I would have to explain.

I can be cautious about what a diploma proves without treating it as though
it proves nothing.

I understand that sentence more easily than I feel it.

## How much does the name promise?

Saying that I studied mathematics describes something that happened. Saying
that I am a mathematician feels like making a claim about whatever might
happen next.

Someone could ask me something.

Even alone, I can imagine the question I would not know how to answer. I do
not need an actual person to ask it. I already know enough about the size of
the subject to supply questions that would defeat me.

Perhaps this is where the name becomes difficult. I hear more inside it than
I could possibly demonstrate in a sentence. It seems to include familiarity
with subjects I have not studied, ease with ideas I still find difficult, and
a kind of intellectual readiness I do not consistently feel.

What exactly am I claiming when I say it?

That I have an education in the field? That I work with its ideas? That I can
contribute something? Or that I should be able to meet any part of it without
being lost?

These claims ask for different evidence. If I leave them mixed together, I
can meet one and still feel dishonest because I have not met another.

I do not need to settle a universal definition of who gets to be called a
mathematician. People use the word in different ways. But I need to understand
what I require of myself when I hear it. Otherwise, I am trying to satisfy a
condition I have never stated clearly enough to examine.

## The people I compare myself with

I compare myself with people at the top of the fields I care about.

There is much to learn from doing that. Their work makes possibilities
visible. An argument can show me a level of understanding I want to develop.
Something they make can change what I think is worth attempting. I do not
want to stop looking closely at excellent work just because the comparison
can be uncomfortable.

But being inspired by someone's understanding and using it to decide whether
my own understanding counts are different uses of the same encounter.

If someone knows much more mathematics than I do, that difference may be
real. I do not need to protect myself by pretending otherwise. The question
is what follows from it. Does their greater ability tell me where I could
learn, or does it mean I am pretending to belong to the field?

Somewhere in the comparison, *good enough to call myself this* becomes
difficult to separate from *as good as the people I most admire*.

Then even substantial knowledge can feel insufficient. I can acknowledge
that I know something and immediately place it beside someone who knows it
more deeply. The comparison is accurate in one respect and still unfair in
what I ask it to decide. Another person's excellence cannot tell me, on its
own, whether I am misrepresenting my own ability.

There is also a question I find uncomfortable: would I apply the same
requirement to someone else?

Would I hear about their education, look at what they can do, and then refuse
to count it because a much stronger person exists? Would I expect them to
reach the top of a field before they were allowed to say they worked in it?

I need to answer that honestly. If my standard changes when the person is
me, calling it rigor does not settle whether it is fair.

## The feeling survives a change of subject

Mathematics gives this difficulty its clearest form for me, but changing
fields does not remove it.

I feel it in AI, in software engineering, and in electrical engineering.
There is evidence that I
can do these things, and there is still a private reluctance to let that
evidence become a description of me.

The evidence has limits, of course. Being able to do one kind of work does
not establish competence in every other kind. A successful implementation
does not answer every theoretical question. Understanding a method does not
mean knowing how to build every system that might use it. I would be making
a mistake if I treated one success as permission to claim everything nearby.

But I can make the opposite mistake too. I can ask a particular achievement
to establish so much that it is guaranteed to seem insufficient.

Then I am left with an odd arrangement. I can say that I did the work. I can
accept that the evidence exists. But saying what that suggests about my
ability feels like a step I am not entitled to take.

Perhaps I distrust that step because I do not want to become someone whose
confidence travels much farther than his understanding. I care about the
difference. I want the words I use about myself to be accurate.

Accuracy should also make room for what is there.

If I acknowledge only my limitations, I have not necessarily described myself
more honestly. I have described one part in detail and left the other part
out.

## The gap deserves attention. What else does it deserve?

I do not want to solve this by declaring every doubt an imposter feeling.
Sometimes I am unprepared. Sometimes there is something I should understand
before I continue. Feeling better about myself would not repair that.

What I want is to be more exact about the demand a gap makes.

Suppose I cannot explain a step in a proof. I may need to return to a
definition, work through an example, or discover an assumption I missed.
There is a question I can pursue. I can eventually check whether I understand
the step better.

If I turn the same difficulty into “Am I really a mathematician?”, the task
changes. Understanding the step may no longer be enough. There will be
another proof, another subject, another person whose knowledge makes mine
look small. I have attached a much larger uncertainty to something that
initially asked me to learn a particular thing.

That example is the distinction I want to keep in view. A real limitation
can require work without requiring a verdict on everything I have become.

The struggle is that knowing this does not automatically change how the
limitation feels. I can explain why the larger conclusion does not follow
and still find it convincing when it concerns me. The doubt persists beside
the explanation.

This is why another person listing my qualifications back to me would not
quite answer the problem. I have not forgotten them. I am having trouble
deciding what I am allowed to conclude from them.

## Evidence I could actually use

Perhaps the question needs to become smaller before I can answer it honestly.

What am I claiming I can do?

For a particular mathematical argument, I can ask whether I understand the
definitions, whether the steps follow, and whether I can account for the
assumptions. Someone else can examine the reasoning and find what I missed.
For a piece of software, I can ask what it is supposed to do, how it behaves,
and what has actually been checked. The evidence will be different because
the claims are different.

These questions do not ask me to feel impressive. They ask me to make an
account of the work that can be examined and corrected.

They also leave room for an honest limit. I can know enough for one task and
need help with another. I can have a substantial education and still need to
study something fundamental. I can be capable of learning a missing idea
without pretending I already know it.

None of that guarantees that I will feel comfortable with the name. But it
gives me something more useful than asking whether I am enough for an entire
field at once.

There are three questions here that I keep bringing too close together:

What can I do?

What do I claim I can do?

Do I feel like the person those answers describe?

The first two can be made precise. The third may take longer. I do not want
to force agreement by inflating what I know. I also do not want the feeling
to have the final word about evidence it has not answered.

## What would I let count?

I still hesitate.

Writing this does not give me the missing mathematics. It does not make me
as good as the people I compare myself with, and it does not remove the
distance between being able to do something and feeling entitled to say so.

But it makes me less certain that another qualification would settle the
question. If I cannot say what evidence I would accept, I cannot reasonably
expect the next achievement to persuade me.

I want to keep studying. There are things I do not understand because I have
not learned them, things I need to revisit, and things difficult enough that
understanding them would be meaningful work. I want to give those questions
the attention they deserve.

I also want to be able to recognize what I have already learned without
feeling that recognition is a promise never to be confused again.

My degrees cannot answer every question someone could ask me. The work I can
do cannot make me capable of every task. I can accept those limits. What I
am trying to understand is why the limits so often feel like the whole
description.

For now, I can say something smaller than “I have finally proved myself.”

I studied mathematics. I earned the degrees. There are things I understand
and things I can do because of that work. There is also a great deal I do
not know.

I am trying to let the last sentence stand without making it erase the
others.
